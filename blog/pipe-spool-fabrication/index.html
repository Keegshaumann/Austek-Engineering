Pipe spool fabrication: what to build off site, and what to weld in place

Austek Engineering CC · Last updated 15 November 2026

Build the pipework as spools, away from the running line, wherever you can. Leave only the joints that have to be measured and closed in position for the plant. That's the short answer. The rest of this guide is how to decide where that split falls on your job, and what we need from you to plan it.

What we mean by a spool

To us, a spool is a length of pipework, with its bends, branches and flanges, that's built and checked as a unit before it's fitted into the line. Our process section sets out how a fabricated pipe spool comes together: cut and prepared, fitted up and tacked, a TIG root pass purged where the specification calls for it, filled and capped, then a visual inspection, a dimensional check and handover before it goes live.

Every step on that list is easier to do well when the spool isn't hanging off a live pipe rack.

Why welding off the line gives you a better joint

A spool on stands can be turned. TWI, the welding research body, explains that when the pipe can be rotated, the welder stays put and the weld is made on top, the most basic pipe welding position. When the pipe is fixed in place, the welder has to work around it, and TWI says the work gets harder the further the position moves from rotated towards fixed.

That matters for your specification as well as for the weld. The welder's certificate has to cover the position the joint is actually welded in. Our guide to what a coded welder is puts it plainly: a test welded flat proves very little about overhead work.

Then there's the weather and the draughts. TWI states that MIG welding can't be done outdoors without enclosures, because the shielding gas has to be protected from the wind. Our TIG vs MIG guide covers why the same care applies to a TIG gas shield and to any purge inside the pipe. Off the line, the joint can be sheltered. In a plant room with doors, extraction fans and a breeze through the roof sheeting, it's a bigger job.

And there's the line itself. Work done off site doesn't need the plant stopped while it's being built. As our FAQ answer on site and off-site work says, fabrication done off site and then installed is usually the faster route where a plant is running or a shutdown window is tight.

What still gets welded in place

Some joints can only be made where the pipe ends up. The usual ones:

  • the tie-ins, where new pipework meets the existing line
  • closing joints between spools, where the last gap is measured and cut in position
  • anything routed through a space too tight to lift a finished section into
  • repairs to pipework that stays where it is

On these, the length is set by what's actually on the plant. Our FAQ makes the point about quoting older plant: the as-built rarely matches the drawing, and a site visit to measure beats working from assumptions. The same goes for planning spools. Where it's practical, we'd rather measure the tie-in points ourselves, with the plant running, than build a spool to a drawing and find out at installation that a flange sits somewhere else. Ask your fabricator how they plan to close each run.

Breakdown repairs, installation and maintenance are done plant-side. So the question to settle early is how much of the work can move away from the line.

The same thinking for steelwork

Platforms, walkways and supports follow the same logic. Frames, stairs and handrail sections can be built and checked away from the plant and then lifted in. What's left on site is setting out, fixing to the existing structure and the connections that have to suit the steel that's already there. If your structure is old, measure it before anything is cut.

What we need from you to plan it

To split a job sensibly between spools and site welds, we need enough information to decide which joints can be made off site before we arrive. The list in our FAQ for quoting covers most of it:

  1. Drawings, a marked-up photograph or a description of the run, including where it meets the existing line.
  2. Materials and grades, and whether certificates are needed.
  3. Where the work is, and what's involved in getting access, including whether a section can be lifted in.
  4. Timing, particularly if installation has to fit a shutdown window.
  5. Any code or specification the pipework has to meet.
  6. Who signs the work off, and against what.
  7. Access to measure, while the plant is still running.

If installation falls inside a planned outage, our guide on how to prepare for a plant shutdown covers what to close out before the window opens. For stainless lines, the guide to choosing a stainless grade covers purging and cleaning up the weld, which applies whether the joint is made off site or in place.

Questions worth asking your fabricator

  • Which joints will be made off site, and which in place?
  • Is the welder on the site joints qualified for the position those joints will be welded in?
  • How will the gas shield and purge be protected on the site welds?
  • Where is the closing cut on each run, and who measures it?
  • How is each spool checked before it leaves, and how is it checked again after it's fitted?

Good answers to these are specific to your plant. Vague ones are worth pushing on before the job is quoted.
