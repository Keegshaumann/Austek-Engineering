How to prepare for a plant shutdown

Austek Engineering CC · Last updated 23 September 2026

A plant shutdown is decided before the plant stops, not during it. The work that fits inside the window is the work that was measured, drawn, quoted, permitted and pre-fabricated in advance. Everything discovered after the line goes down competes for hours that were already allocated. Preparation is not admin around the job — on a shutdown, preparation largely is the job.

Why does preparation matter more on a shutdown than on ordinary work?

On a shutdown, the constraint is time, and time cannot be bought back at any price. On normal maintenance, a delay pushes a completion date. On a shutdown, a delay either pushes the restart — with production losses behind it — or forces work to be dropped and carried to the next window, sometimes a year away.

That changes what good looks like. A contractor who is fast on the tools but vague on planning is a liability in a shutdown, because the hours are lost long before anyone picks up a grinder: waiting on a permit, waiting on material, waiting on a measurement that could have been taken while the plant was running.

What should be settled before the window opens?

Every question that can be answered with the plant running should be answered with the plant running. The shutdown window is for work that physically cannot happen any other way — breaking into live lines, lifting over running equipment, entering vessels.

Worth having closed out in advance:

  • Scope, in writing, line by line. Vague scope is what turns into overrun.
  • Site measurements taken and checked. Not scaled off a drawing that may not match what was built.
  • Materials ordered and delivered. Including the right grade, with certificates where the specification calls for them.
  • Pre-fabrication done off site. Spools, supports and structures built and checked in a workshop, so the window is spent installing rather than fabricating.
  • Permits, inductions and safety files in place. These reliably take longer than anyone expects, and they cannot be rushed on the day.
  • Isolation and lock-out agreed with the plant. Who isolates what, and when it is safe to start.
  • Access sorted. Scaffolding, lifting, and how the work gets to where it needs to be.
  • A written sequence. What happens on each day, and what has to finish before the next thing can start.

What does a fabricator need from you to quote shutdown work properly?

A shutdown quote is only as good as the information behind it, and the gaps get filled with assumptions. The more of the following a contractor has, the closer the number will be to what the job actually costs — and the less room there is for a variation later.

What to send, and why it changes the quote
What to send Why it matters
Drawings or sketches Sets the scope. Even a marked-up photograph beats a verbal description.
Materials and grades Drives cost and lead time, and decides whether certificates are needed.
The window — dates and hours Determines crew size and whether the work needs shifts.
Applicable code or specification Decides which welder and procedure qualifications apply.
Site access and induction requirements Often the single largest source of lost hours on day one.
Whether a site visit is possible first Almost always pays for itself. Measuring beats assuming.
Who signs off, and against what Avoids finished work sitting idle waiting for an inspection nobody booked.

What goes wrong most often?

Most shutdown overruns trace back to a handful of avoidable causes, and nearly all of them are decided before the window opens. None of these are exotic — which is exactly why they are worth checking off deliberately rather than assuming they are handled.

  • Scope found after isolation. Corrosion or damage nobody could see while the plant was running. Some of this is unavoidable; the answer is a contingency in the plan, not surprise on the day.
  • As-built does not match the drawing. Extremely common on older plant. Site measurement is the only defence.
  • Material arrives late or wrong. Ordering early is cheap; a line waiting on a flange is not.
  • Permits and inductions not cleared in advance. A full crew standing at a gate is the most expensive way to start.
  • Sign-off not arranged. Completed work waiting on an inspector who was never booked.
  • Too many trades in one space. A sequence that reads well on paper but cannot physically be worked.

What should happen at handover?

Handover is part of the work, not an afterthought once the tools are packed. On a shutdown it is also the last chance to catch something before the plant is live again and the cost of access goes back up.

At minimum: a walk of the completed work with the plant's representative, any inspection or testing the specification calls for, the paperwork that has to go into the plant's records, and a written list of anything that could not be completed and why. That last item is the one most often skipped and the one most worth having — it is what the next window's scope gets built from.

When is a shutdown the wrong time to do the work?

Not every job belongs in a shutdown, and putting the wrong work into one is expensive. Shutdown hours are the most costly hours on the plant's calendar, so anything that does not strictly need the plant stopped is usually better done outside the window.

If the work can be pre-fabricated off site, done in a running-plant isolation, or completed during a routine stoppage, that is almost always the cheaper route. It is worth asking a contractor directly which parts of a proposed scope genuinely need the window — and being slightly wary of one who says all of it does without having looked.
